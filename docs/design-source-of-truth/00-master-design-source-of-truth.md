# Master Design Source Of Truth

## Purpose

This is the design authority for the project. It defines what the game should feel like, what the first vertical slice must prove, and what implementation work should optimize for.

The current mechanics are good enough to preserve. The reset is visual and experiential: the store needs to stop reading as a prototype space and start reading as an early-2000s specialty video game retailer.

## Core Fantasy

The player is opening and operating a small independent video game store in the early 2000s.

The successful player reaction is:

> This feels like a real game store. I understand how the business works. I can see how to grow it. I want to keep building it.

The player is not:

- a major retailer
- a franchise owner
- a regional chain
- an online storefront

The store begins with:

- limited cash
- limited inventory
- limited shelving
- limited distributor access
- limited reputation

It should feel new, underfunded, understocked, promising, and operational. It should not feel empty, abandoned, fully developed, sterile, or modern.

## Era

The vertical slice starts in 2002-2004.

The starting platform set is fictional:

- Nova
- Vertex
- Prism
- Pocket

All platforms, products, publishers, retailers, and brands are fictional. The game must be instantly recognizable without copying real companies, consoles, games, retailers, publishers, logos, or packaging.

Future era expansion may introduce:

- Pocket Advance
- Horizon
- Pulse
- later legacy/used-inventory transitions

Future-era content is not part of the first vertical slice unless explicitly needed as locked catalog preview.

## Store Personality

The store is:

- enthusiast-owned
- merchandise heavy
- colorful
- busy
- imperfect
- slightly crowded
- full of personality
- built by gamers for gamers

The store is not:

- a modern electronics retailer
- a department store
- a minimalist showroom
- a sterile corporate environment
- a collectibles shop with games on the side

The focus remains games. Hardware, accessories, guides, media, and collectibles support the game-store fantasy; they do not replace it.

## Design Pillars

1. Atmosphere beats breadth.
   When forced to choose between more atmosphere and more simulation depth, prefer atmosphere.

2. Fictional but recognizable.
   Everything should read as period-authentic without direct real-world brands.

3. Growth must be visible.
   The store starts limited and transforms through better inventory, better fixtures, better access, and stronger merchandising.

4. Inventory access matters.
   Money alone should never unlock everything. Reputation, distributor trust, store quality, progression, and special opportunities gate access.

5. The store sells the fantasy before UI does.
   The player should understand entrance, platform walls, used games, new releases, demo area, trade-in counter, and checkout from the environment.

6. Current mechanics stay stable.
   Movement, interaction, receiving, pricing, stocking, register, trade-ins, ordering, fixture placement, save/load, and day summary are foundations to preserve while the design reset changes the visual/experience target.

## Current Implementation Stance

The current art-kit route is not the design target by itself. It is an implementation attempt that helped separate visible art-kit instances from raw graybox work.

Going forward, implementation should be judged against this design source of truth:

- 2002-2004 independent specialty game store.
- underfunded but functional opening condition.
- dense period-authentic merchandising over empty showroom space.
- fictional platforms and products.
- visible growth path.
- asset roadmap driven by the 300-object inventory.

Do not broaden customer visuals, decoration breadth, hidden narrative, multi-era catalog visuals, or external playtest packaging until the opening store loop and first environment read meet this source of truth.
