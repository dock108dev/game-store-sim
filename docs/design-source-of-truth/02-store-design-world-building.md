# Store Design And World Building

## Core Design Goal

The player should immediately understand:

> This feels exactly like a game store in 2004.

They should understand this without seeing real consoles, real games, real companies, or real retailers.

## Product Mix

The store remains game-first.

Target mature inventory distribution:

- 60% video games
- 15% hardware
- 10% accessories
- 10% guides, magazines, and gaming media
- 5% collectibles and novelty items

The store should never become primarily a collectibles shop.

## Layout Philosophy

Customer flow should encourage browsing.

Preferred flow:

1. Entrance
2. Promotional displays
3. New releases
4. Main browsing area
5. Platform sections
6. Used games
7. Checkout counter

The player should be encouraged to walk the whole store before leaving.

## Required Zones

### Trade-In Counter

The trade-in counter is one of the most important locations in the game. Many systems originate here: used inventory acquisition, condition review, pricing decisions, customer trust, and optional hidden anomalies.

### Used Games Wall

Large, visible, and prominent. Used inventory is a major business driver and should visibly grow.

### New Releases Wall

High traffic and frequently refreshed. It should carry the sense that the store is current even when the business is small.

### Platform Sections

Dedicated sections for major platforms. The player and customers should be able to visually parse Nova, Vertex, Prism, and Pocket areas.

### Demo Area

Required. It gives the store life and creates a gathering point. The vertical slice needs one kiosk and one display screen.

### Hardware Section

Consoles, accessories, controllers, memory expansion devices, and related packaging.

### Bargain Bin

High interaction and low value. It should feel like a treasure-hunting area.

### Guides And Media

Important for period authenticity. Strategy guides, magazines, and gaming media should be present in the slice even if mechanically lightweight at first.

## Era Progression

### Early Era: 2002-2004

Primary platforms:

- Nova
- Vertex
- Prism
- Pocket

Visual characteristics:

- large game walls
- strategy guides
- memory expansion cards
- wired accessories
- CRT demo stations
- dense shelving
- heavy focus on physical software

### Mid Era: 2005-2007

Future expansion platforms:

- Pocket Advance
- Horizon
- Pulse

Visual changes:

- launch displays
- larger preorder campaigns
- expanded hardware sections
- more demo kiosks
- growing customer traffic

### Late Era: 2008-2011

Future visual changes:

- mature Horizon and Pulse sections
- Pocket Color
- legacy platforms moving into used inventory
- larger used section
- more accessories
- gift cards
- premium display fixtures
- bigger promotional campaigns

These later eras are roadmap context, not first-slice scope.

## Environmental Storytelling

The store should tell stories without dialogue.

Examples:

- empty shelf after a major release
- nearly sold-out preorder board
- rare item behind the counter
- aging clearance section
- outdated poster that was never removed
- employee recommendation shelf
- customer request board
- trade-in tote with half-processed games
- handwritten release reminders

Environmental storytelling should communicate history, activity, and ownership.

## Customer Archetypes

The store should visually and behaviorally support:

- Enthusiast: checks new releases immediately.
- Collector: browses used inventory first.
- Parent: looks for recommendations.
- Bargain Hunter: searches discount inventory.
- Trade-In Customer: arrives carrying products and heads to the counter.
- Browser: explores extensively and may leave without purchasing.

Customers should help validate the layout: if their behavior does not naturally use the zones, the store design is not working.

## Hidden Narrative Boundaries

The game contains a hidden optional mystery, but it must never interfere with the primary store simulation.

Rules:

1. Entirely optional.
2. Never required for progression.
3. Discoverable through normal gameplay.
4. Grounded in gaming culture.
5. No supernatural elements.
6. No world-ending conspiracies.
7. Multiple possible outcomes.
8. Resolution occurs after years of in-game time.
9. Store operation continues after resolution.
10. Future playthroughs may contain variations.

Discovery channels:

- trade-ins
- inventory anomalies
- rare products
- customer interactions
- employee behavior
- industry rumors
- store records

At first these events should look like harmless background details.

## Art Direction Rule

When forced to choose between more atmosphere and more simulation depth, prefer atmosphere.

The goal is to recreate the feeling of spending an hour browsing a game store after school, discovering something unexpected, and leaving with a game the player did not plan to buy.
