# Vision

## High Concept

Run a mall-era game store from the counter up. Buy used games, sell new releases, restock shelves, manage trade-ins, deal with customers, configure displays, expand the shop, decorate the space, and slowly discover that some inventory, customers, invoices, and backroom opportunities do not add up.

The game should feel like operating an actual small specialty retailer, not like watching a tycoon dashboard. The player should physically touch the store: open boxes, scan games, price items, place cases on shelves, work the register, answer customer questions, and decide when to cut corners.

## Design Pillars

- Tactile retail work: the minute-to-minute play is built around hands-on actions, not abstract buttons.
- Customer stories through transactions: customer interactions should reveal wants, budgets, habits, scams, loyalty, awkwardness, and rumors.
- Inventory is the game board: every item has cost, value, rarity, demand, condition, and shelf presence.
- Store layout matters: placement affects traffic, browsing, theft risk, impulse buys, queue flow, and staff efficiency.
- Progression through capability: upgrades should unlock new workflows, categories, tools, services, risks, and responsibilities.
- Hidden consequences: the suspicious thread exists under normal systems and can be missed, exploited, reported, or escalated.

## Player Fantasy

The player starts as the owner/operator of a small used game shop in a mall-adjacent retail strip. They are undercapitalized, doing everything manually, and trying to become the local place for used games, new releases, consoles, controllers, memory cards, strategy guides, repairs, launch nights, and retro collectors.

The fantasy is not pure nostalgia. The store should feel warm and familiar, but also operationally stressful: thin margins, picky customers, bad trades, questionable suppliers, rent pressure, shrinkage, weird regulars, launch-day chaos, and the temptation to take easy money.

## Scope Boundaries

In scope:

- First-person store operation.
- Customer conversations and negotiations.
- Register, pricing, trade-in, checkout, refunds, preorders, and layaway-like systems.
- Stock ordering, receiving, sorting, shelf stocking, backroom storage, and shrinkage.
- Store setup, fixture placement, decoration, signage, lighting, and expansion.
- Data-driven game products, categories, conditions, rarity, demand, and market volatility.
- Optional hidden narrative tied to invoices, customers, suspicious goods, cash payments, and backroom choices.

Out of scope for the first serious build:

- Real licensed brands, real games, real console names, or real retailer branding.
- Multiplayer.
- Streamer chat, facecam overlays, video player UI, or YouTube/Twitch framing.
- Full city simulation outside the store.
- Complex employee management before the solo-owner loop is fun.
- Combat, stealth action, or crime gameplay as a primary genre.

## Tone

The tone should be grounded, dry, and slightly strange. The store can be funny because retail is funny, not because every customer is a joke. The hidden thread should feel plausible and unsettling, not cartoon villainy.

## Working Comparable Set

The screenshots point toward:

- Physical construction and fixture placement like a shop simulator.
- Product pricing, ordering, and shelf placement loops from small-business sims.
- Collectible valuation and customer offer moments, translated into used games, retro hardware, boxed editions, accessories, and sealed inventory.
- A first-person interaction layer instead of a top-down management board.

The game should not clone any one reference. The distinctive hook is the used-game retail economy: trade-ins, condition judgment, collector demand, platform cycles, preorders, used margins, suspicious inventory, and local customer reputation.
